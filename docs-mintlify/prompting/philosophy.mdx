Generic prompts produce AI-looking output. "A stunning ethereal portrait, high-quality, 8K, masterpiece" is the prompt of someone who has never written one. Every token in that string nudges the model toward the average of its training distribution, which is the polished, oversaturated, beauty-filtered look that reads as AI in the first 200ms of a scroll. This page is the doctrine for writing prompts that don't.

## The lexical tells

These tokens flag a prompt as AI-by-AI. They survive into the output as visible style traits.

| Banned token | What it produces |
|---|---|
| `powerful`, `stunning`, `ethereal`, `breathtaking` | Generic over-grading, halo-bloom, no specific register |
| `high-quality`, `8K`, `masterpiece`, `award-winning` | Smooth oversharpened skin, beauty-filter look |
| `cinematic` (alone) | Tom-Ford chiaroscuro, marble + tuxedo, ARRI Alexa defaults |
| `professional photo`, `photorealistic` | Stock-photography register, no point of view |
| `beautiful lighting`, `perfect composition` | Symmetric, magazine-cover, lifeless |

The fix is not to negate them. `NO ethereal, NO stunning` doesn't work — gemini and the kling text encoder don't reliably suppress negated style tokens. The fix is to replace them with **specific** alternatives the model can lock onto.

## The replacement vocabulary

The single highest-impact swap is **named gear over adjectives**. The model's training corpus is full of EXIF metadata and forum threads where photographers cite their setup. Naming the gear pulls the output into the basin of those specific images instead of the basin of "professional photo on Instagram".

```text
# Bad
A stunning portrait of a young woman, photorealistic, beautiful natural light.

# Good
Black woman, late 20s, soft warm features, shoulder-length thin natural locs.
Shot on Sony A7 IV + Sigma 35mm f/1.4 ART, Kodak Portra 400 film stock.
Soft window light from screen-left, single source, no fill.
Hyperreal NOT glossy, naturalistic candid not staged. Slight film grain,
visible skin pores, flyaway hair at the temple, slight facial asymmetry.
```

Every phrase in the good prompt is doing work:

- **`Sony A7 IV + Sigma 35mm f/1.4 ART`** — locks the focal length and sensor. The model has trained on thousands of correctly-EXIF-tagged 35mm shots; "DSLR" is too weak.
- **`Kodak Portra 400`** — single highest-impact token in the photoreal-human vocabulary. It pulls the color science off the modern oversaturated cinema-LUT default and onto a documented warm-skin film stock. Validated across noski-people, venom-bodywash, glitter-cream projects per [`photoreal-humans.md`](https://github.com/alecs5am/ralphy/blob/main/docs/playbooks/art-director/photoreal-humans.md).
- **`Soft window light from screen-left, single source`** — "single source" is load-bearing. Multi-source studio defaults are where beauty-filter outputs live.
- **`Hyperreal NOT glossy, naturalistic candid not staged`** — these two phrases verbatim are the strongest anti-AI-slop tokens in gemini-3-pro's vocabulary. The "NOT X" framing works for these specific style words (it doesn't for ethereal / stunning / etc).
- **`Visible skin pores, flyaway hair, slight asymmetry`** — defects. The AI default is poreless, symmetric, perfectly arranged. Calling out the defects explicitly inverts the default.

## The anti-AI-slop block

Append this verbatim to the negatives on every photoreal-human gen. The block comes from the art-director playbook and is the difference between a usable anchor and an obviously-AI face:

```text
NO glossy beauty-filter skin, NO enlarged anime-eyes, NO smoothed jawline-reshape,
NO airbrush, NO uniform porelessness, NO oversharpened eyelashes, NO catchlight-bomb,
NO Tom-Ford chiaroscuro, NO TV-commercial register, NO Apple-keynote rim-light.
```

User feedback "too AI-perfectly-perfect" on noski and glitter-cream traces directly to skipping this block. Source: [`art-director/photoreal-humans.md`](https://github.com/alecs5am/ralphy/blob/main/docs/playbooks/art-director/photoreal-humans.md).

## The 5-cue checklist for photoreal humans

Every photoreal-human image prompt MUST carry all five. Not three, not four:

1. **Specific real camera body + prime lens.** "Sony A7 IV + Sigma 35mm f/2.8 ART", "Leica M11 + Summicron 50mm", "Canon R5 + RF 85mm f/1.2". Generic "DSLR" drifts to glossy.
2. **Specific real film stock or sensor profile.** "Kodak Portra 400", "Fuji 400H", "Cinestill 800T", "natural digital sensor no grading". Highest-impact single token.
3. **Ambient single-source light, not studio.** "Soft window light from the left", "open shade midday", "kitchen overhead with bounce". The word "single" matters.
4. **The two phrases verbatim:** `hyperreal NOT glossy` and `naturalistic candid not staged`.
5. **Identity restated inline.** Even with `--ref`, restate the identity in prose. Gemini's i2v sometimes ignores the ref when the prompt body drifts.

The TV-commercial register (Tom-Ford chiaroscuro, ARRI Alexa, Cooke S4) is the default agent instinct and it is the wrong default for UGC. Venom-bodywash-001 burned ~$3 before the user manually pointed at noski-people-001 as the correct register.

## Why "candid not staged" works and "natural" doesn't

The word "natural" appears in roughly every stock-photography description on the internet. Asking for "natural lighting" gets you the average of stock-natural, which is the soft-key-with-bounce that every commercial uses.

"Candid not staged" is a different idiomatic phrase, more often seen in documentary-photography contexts. It pulls the output toward street-photo and reportage corpora. Same logic for "hyperreal NOT glossy" — "realistic" is too generic; "hyperreal" without the negation drifts toward CGI; the pair together is the narrow band that works.

This is the general principle: **pick tokens that are specific to the corpus you want, not the corpus you don't.** Asking the model to avoid X by saying "not X" is unreliable; asking it to chase Y by naming Y precisely is reliable.

## When generic is fine

Not every prompt needs the full anti-slop treatment.

- **Brand product hero shot** — the product is the subject. Glamour register is correct. `cinematic commercial, color-graded teal/orange` is fine.
- **Motion-graphic / abstract** — no human face to read as AI-perfect. Style tokens like "neon", "geometric", "vector-clean" are unambiguous.
- **Brainrot character / cartoon** — the whole point is that it looks unreal. Anti-slop language fights the aesthetic.

The doctrine fires hardest on **photoreal humans** and **everyday lifestyle scenes** where the viewer's brain is looking for "is this real?". The minute you're showing a human face at ≥20% of the frame, the 5-cue checklist is mandatory.

## The "deliberate fakeness" exception

Some aesthetics — Old Spice's intentionally-rubber creature, Wes Anderson's flat painted backdrops — are obviously fake on purpose. These need a different vocabulary: matte rubber, kelly-green-not-natural, visible seams, stiff posture, Muppet googly eyes. All 5 cues required or it reads as AI failure instead of intentional craft. See [Old Spice VFX cookbook](/cookbook/oldspice-vfx).

The rule: if the brief says "make it obviously fake," lean **into** the artifice with specific named-corpus references (Muppets, B-movie creature features, claymation), not into the photoreal vocabulary inverted.

## Broadcast-realism: a worked example of the principle

A "caught on TV" trend prompt (sports, news, audience-cam) defaults to 9:16 portrait if you let it. Real broadcast cameras shoot 16:9, and a 9:16-cropped broadcast read produces AI-uncanny content because no real broadcast camera shoots that way. The fix is to pick **1:1 square** as the aspect ratio for these shots — square crops convincingly fit a 9:16 feed canvas while preserving the 16:9 broadcast geometry, and the eye reads them as authentic.

This is the same principle as the named-gear rule applied at the composition level: pick the format the actual source corpus uses, not the format your platform suggests. See the [broadcast-square cookbook](/cookbook/broadcast-square).
