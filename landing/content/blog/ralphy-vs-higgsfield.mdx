# Ralphy vs Higgsfield / Supercomputer — what AI-native teams actually need

Marketing tools shipping AI features have split into two camps.

**Camp one** is the hosted studio: Higgsfield, Supercomputer, the next dozen seed-funded clones. You log in, type a brief, get a video. Their pitch is convenience. Their business model is owning the pipeline.

**Camp two** is the open-source CLI you run yourself. Ralphy lives here. The pitch is the opposite: you keep the pipeline. The studio is software you can read, fork, and pin.

If you ship one TikTok a quarter, camp one is fine. If your team ships ten a week — or you intend to — the difference compounds fast. Below is the honest version of what teams discover six months in.

## Five things you actually own in camp two

### 1. Your provider keys, your provider rates

Hosted studios run on credits. Higgsfield's image credit ≠ Higgsfield's video credit ≠ the upgrade tier you got grandfathered into. The price they pay OpenAI or fal.ai is not the price they charge you, and they keep both sides opaque on purpose.

Ralphy never holds a credit. The only secrets it needs are your `OPENROUTER_API_KEY` and `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY`. Every generation is a direct, logged call against your account, at the published list price. When Sora-2 drops and is half the cost of Veo, you swap one line in `MODELS.md` — no Ralphy update required, no waiting for the platform to "support" it.

### 2. The generations log, in plain JSON, on your disk

Each project under Ralphy writes a `workspace/projects/<id>/logs/generations.jsonl`. Every model call: input prompt, output URI, latency, cost, model id. Append-only. Greppable. Not behind a "View History" tab that paginates twenty entries and times out.

Six months in, this is the file that lets you answer questions a CMO actually asks:

- Which prompt template produced our three highest-performing reels?
- What did we spend on hands-and-eyes regens in Q1?
- Show me every scene where we used `kling-v3.0-pro --audio` for Russian VO.

In Higgsfield or Supercomputer, those questions are either a support ticket or impossible.

### 3. No "your tokens expired" surprise

Pre-paid AI credit pools have a known failure mode: they expire. Sometimes after 30 days, sometimes after a "subscription pause". The credits you saved for a campaign launch can vanish because billing rolled over while the legal review was happening.

Ralphy doesn't have a token concept. There is no Ralphy account. You pay OpenRouter and ElevenLabs directly, on their cadence, with their rules — which are at least documented and not unilaterally rewriteable by a Series-A startup chasing margin.

### 4. Reference fidelity that lives in your repo, not in someone's product

The hard part of producing UGC isn't generating *a* video — it's generating one that matches a brand. Higgsfield's "style transfer" is a closed black box: you upload three reference images, get something back, can't tell what features were extracted, can't reuse them next month.

Ralphy's reference pipeline is six discrete CLI verbs (`ralphy ref pull → frames → transcript → vision → audio-describe → blueprint`) that write structured JSON. Master shots, persona descriptors, camera-grammar notes — all checked into your repo next to the project. When the campaign extends to round 2 in eight months, you re-run the same blueprint instead of paying for a "Vision Analysis" credit pack again.

This is also why Ralphy refuses to render named real entities ("Elon Musk", "Coca-Cola can") without a checked-in reference image. The gate is offline and reads from `cli/lib/eval/refs.ts`. Higgsfield's content policy lives on a slide deck and is enforced by trust.

### 5. Determinism the rendering pipeline can prove

Ralphy uses Remotion for the final composition pass. Remotion is just React + a headless Chromium renderer — meaning your final mp4 is the output of code you can read, version, and test like any other module. Caption timing is a function call. Transition curves are TypeScript. Music ducking is a constant.

Hosted studios ship a render engine you cannot inspect. When the "magic" subtly changes between releases — different easing on the title card, a new caption font — your client sees the difference before you do.

## What hosted studios actually have on us

Honesty: there are three things Higgsfield and Supercomputer do better today.

1. **Zero setup.** You don't have to install bun, get a `ralphy` binary, set two env vars, run `ralphy doctor`. If your team writes zero code, that gap is real.
2. **One billing relationship.** OpenRouter + ElevenLabs is two cards, two invoices, two TOS pages. A studio is one.
3. **A web UI that designers can click in.** Ralphy is a CLI. The intended user is an AI coding agent (Claude Code, Cursor, etc.) operating on your behalf. If you don't have agentic workflows yet, the seam is sharper.

If those three matter more to you than the five we listed above, hosted is the right call. If you're an AI-native team that already runs `gh`, `claude`, and a dotfiles repo, the trade-off inverts.

## What "AI-native" looks like in practice

The teams that get the most out of Ralphy share a few habits:

- **They treat prompts like code.** Versioned, reviewed, diff-able. Ralphy puts `prompts.json` and `STORYBOARD.md` in each project — both are checked into git.
- **They let an agent drive the CLI.** `claude` reads `AGENTS.md`, picks the right playbook for the user intent, executes the right `ralphy` verbs, and writes the logs. The human reviews the diff and the mp4.
- **They retrofit their stack instead of replacing it.** Ralphy doesn't replace your Figma, your Notion, your Linear, or your video reviewer. It slots between the brief and the render, leaving the rest of your tooling intact.

If that's the loop you're already in — or planning to be in by Q4 — you don't need a hosted studio. You need a CLI you can pin to a commit, a Slack channel where the team can grep prompt history, and an agent that knows when to refuse to render.
